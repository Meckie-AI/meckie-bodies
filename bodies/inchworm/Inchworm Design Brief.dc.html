Concept views

A retro space caterpillar on a low cream dome. Four plump oval segments alternate sea foam and cream, each with two translucent ribs that glow from LEDs inside, a pair of red TPU prolegs on the belly, and chrome joint rings between them. The red braided harness runs up the back of every segment under brass clips. The head is wider than the body, like a caterpillar's: a cream dome with a sea-foam band, the face in a chrome bezel with a red TPU ring, a small chrome camera hood above it, and two antennae with glowing tips. A glow ring circles the top of the base. Each view can be dragged to orbit.

Alternating axes
J1, J3 and J5 bend in one plane; J2 and J4 bend at right angles to it. Together they let the body curve in any direction, tilt its head sideways and bow down to rest. There is no base turntable, so turning to face you is done by bending the two side joints.
Light at the tip
The head carries only a XIAO ESP32-S3 Sense (camera, face, two mics), the round display and a tiny forward lidar: about {{ headStr }} g. Everything heavy that does not have to move lives in the base, and the J1 servo sits inside the base rather than on the arm.
Spine harness
Seven conductors run the whole length in red braid on the back of each segment: 12 V, GND and servo data for the bus, 5 V, TX and RX for the head, and one data line for the glow LEDs. Each joint has a service loop on the outside of the bend, under a brass clip.

06b

Why these materials

PETG
59% of the printed pieces
Shells and enclosures. Heat tolerance, toughness at a snap hook, and a wide enough window to take a heat-set insert without slumping the boss around it.
CF-nylon
24% of the printed pieces
Structure: the parts that actually carry load. Stiffness and fatigue life where a bracket or a yoke is the thing holding the body together.
TPU 95A
18% of the printed pieces
Anything compliant: tyres, bumpers, feet. Grip and a surface that absorbs a knock instead of transmitting it into the shell.
Why not PLA

This body's servos warn at 65 °C and cut torque at 70 °C. PLA's glass transition is about 55 to 60 °C, which is below the temperature its own thermal protection is still treating as normal. The shell would start going soft while the servos inside it were still inside their rated envelope. PETG softens around 80 to 85 °C, which puts the enclosure above the limits of what it encloses.

Creep. PLA deforms slowly under a steady room-temperature load. This arm holds a pose for hours at a time, with the mass of a head on a moment arm. Over weeks that sags. PETG holds.

Snap fits. Nothing here is glued. Every shell closes on cantilever hooks and latches that flex each time it is opened, and that is the exact geometry PLA fails at. The same goes for the brass inserts every screw lands in: setting one means a 200 °C iron against the wall, and PLA's narrow window lets the boss slump where PETG holds its shape.

Where PLA is genuinely better: it is stiffer, it holds a dimension more tightly, and it prints more easily without supports. If you are making a test fit, a mock-up, or a part that never gets warm and never flexes, print it in PLA. It is the production shells that want PETG.

07

Safety reflex · bows down to rest

{{ s.t }} {{ s.h }} {{ s.d }}
Triggers
No Central heartbeat for 500 ms · any servo at 70 °C or above · input below 13 V (charger sagging or unplugged) · eFuse fault · contact: servo load spike or position error over 6°.
Always-on local reflexes
Chin lidar under 60 mm (a face or hand right there) → stop and back off 20 mm. Contact → stop and drop to 30% torque so it yields when touched.
Rest pose
J1 85°, J2 0°, J3 −35°, J4 0°, J5 −30°: it bows forward off the dome and rests its chin on the desk, about 115 mm high. Found by a collision check of the printed parts; stable with torque off.
Power loss
No battery by design. If the plug is pulled while it is stretched out, the chain sags under its own weight onto the desk; prolegs, ribs and the face ring are TPU-padded. Behaviours keep it in low S-curves most of the time.

Verify before CAD freeze
  • J1 thermal: hold the chain straight out for 20 s and for 60 s; set the firmware hold limit from the result.
  • Joint ranges from a collision sweep of the printed parts: J1 −50° to 95°, J2–J5 about −85° to 35° (the long side is away from the bracket's bed face). Confirm on the real build.
  • Spine harness flex life: 50,000 cycles through all five joints; the braid must sit on the outside of every bend.
  • STS3215 backlash stacked over five joints puts about ±3 mm of wobble at the head. Check that it reads as "creature" and not "loose"; preload with a light spring at J1 if needed.
  • Self-collision limits in the IK: use the swept ranges as hard limits with a 5° margin. The plump shells limit how far it can curl, so its rest pose is a forward bow rather than a tight coil.
  • Power-loss drop: pull the plug at full extension and check that nothing breaks; consider a 2 F supercap so it can curl down first.
  • Head pins: the XIAO uses all 11 header pins; the display backlight is tied on to free a pin for the lidar.
  • No base turntable: confirm turning toward a voice with J2 and J4 alone looks natural, or add a sixth servo at the base (+$22, +60 g).
  • Kid safety: pinch points between segments in tight curls; keep shells overlapping so fingers cannot reach the gaps.
